Long-distance couples who speak different languages: games you can both enjoy

Ahmed Moaz · 17 October 2026

Most game advice for long-distance couples assumes you share a first language. Word games, quizzes and "would you rather" all reward speed in one language, and the partner working in their second one is always half a beat behind. For couples who speak different languages, the fix is to choose games where the words are optional, shared, or deliberately one-sided. I make a small set of free online games that run in a browser, and three kinds of game among them fit that well.

Games with no words at all

Ludo and Snakes and Ladders run on a die, not a vocabulary. Nobody reads a card or types an answer.

Snakes and Ladders asks nothing of either player except to throw, so it is fair between any two people, whatever their language or their mood. It takes about 15 minutes. Ludo adds one choice a turn, which piece to move, and takes 30 to 60 minutes. With only two players it leans further toward luck than it does with four, which suits a couple where one partner is the board-game person.

These are the games for a tired game night, when one of you has spent all day working in the other's language and does not want to translate a single joke.

If you want to try a two-player game in a browser, Board It, the free game site I run, has five that work with two.

Drawing: the picture is shared, the word is not

Draw and Guess is where language becomes part of the fun instead of a handicap. One of you draws, the other types guesses. With two players the drawer earns what the guesser's answer was worth, so on every turn you both want the same thing. I would call it half co-operative.

The host can also add custom words, separated by commas, and with ten or more different words the game can use that list alone. The other player never sees it.

That makes a simple exercise. The partner who speaks the language natively hosts and types a list in it, for the other to learn. When the learner draws, they have to read the word and picture it. When the other partner draws, the learner has to come up with the word and type it.

The matching is forgiving where it should be. Capitals and accents do not count, so a learner without the right accent key still scores. Spelling still matters beyond that: a guess one letter off is only marked as close, and only the guesser is told. I have not tested words in non-Latin alphabets, so try one before you build a whole list around them.

There is some evidence that drawing helps words stick. In 2016, Jeffrey Wammes, Melissa Meade and Myra Fernandes found that people remembered words they drew better than words they wrote out, and drawing quality did not matter (Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, volume 69). Their tests used single English words with students, not couples learning a language, so treat it as a hint rather than a promise.

A quiz in the stronger language

Trivia Tavern's questions are in English. If English is one partner's stronger language, that is a real imbalance, and it is better to say so than to pretend otherwise. Two settings soften it. The host picks the topics before the match, so you can choose subjects the second-language reader knows well. The host can also raise the 20-second answer clock, which gives a slower reader room.

Three things for couples who speak different languages to agree first

  1. Which language the guesses use for this game, and whether you switch next time.
  2. Who hosts. The host sets the topics, the clock and the word list, so take turns.
  3. What counts as a win for the evening. A near miss in a second language can be the high point of the night, even if the game does not score it.

What these games can't do

The site and the quiz are in English. Who's the Spy needs at least three players, so it will not work for two. None of this replaces a lesson. It gives the call something to be about, in a way that does not always favour the same person.
