Icebreaker games online for a first meeting: a ten-minute opening and a host script

Ahmed Moaz · 1 November 2026

The first minutes of a first meeting set a tone that lasts, whether it is a video call with a client or a game night with strangers. People arrive unsure who else is there, whether to turn the camera on, and whether anyone will speak first. Good icebreaker games online solve that without asking anyone to say something personal. In this article I give a ten-minute opening with a script for the host, using two games that can be cut to size.

What goes wrong in the first five minutes?

Three things, usually. Someone waits in silence and decides the meeting is awkward. The host asks everyone to introduce themselves, which turns into a slow queue where each person is thinking about their own turn and not listening to the last one. And the one person who knows everybody chats to them while the rest watch.

An icebreaker that works avoids all three. Everyone is busy from the first second, everyone acts at the same time, and nobody has to be interesting.

What does a ten-minute opening look like?

Here is the shape I would use for a group of up to twelve.

  • Minute 0 to 2: the host welcomes people by name as they arrive and pastes the link in the chat.
  • Minute 2 to 3: the host says the one rule, which is "nobody has to be good at this".
  • Minute 3 to 9: a short game.
  • Minute 9 to 10: the host thanks the group and moves to the agenda.

For the short game, I like a drawing round. Draw and Guess seats 2 to 12, and the host can set two rounds with 40-second drawings. With five players that is 5 × 2 × 40 = 400 seconds, which is under seven minutes of drawing at most. A quiz works as well for up to eight people, as Trivia Tavern lets the host pick one of nine categories or Mixed.

How should the host explain it?

Keep the script to three sentences. For a drawing round, I would say: "One person gets a word and draws it. The rest of us type guesses. The drawings will be terrible, and that is the point." Then start. People learn the rules faster by playing one drawing than by hearing them twice.

Do not read out the scoring. Scores matter less than the laughter, and long explanations raise nerves. If someone asks, say that quicker guesses score more, and move on.

How do you choose words that are safe for strangers?

The host can type in custom words that players cannot see. For a first meeting, I would use plain objects and places: a bridge, a teapot, a mountain, a bicycle. Avoid anything tied to politics, religion, age or a person's job. Also avoid in-jokes, since a new member cannot be in on one.

If the meeting has a theme, such as a product launch, a few words from that theme help a new group feel they belong. Keep them gentle and general.

How do you get people in without friction?

Send the link in the invitation and again at the start of the call. Sign-in on Boardit, the site I make on my own, is a typed name, so nobody needs to remember a password, and there is no download. The games are free online games, which suits a meeting with no budget line. That matters when the first meeting includes people from outside your company, who may be on a laptop where they cannot install software.

Tell people the camera is their choice. A guest can play with the camera off and still take part fully.

What do you do if the group is bigger than twelve?

Split into two rooms and give each a host. The pairing works: the main host runs one room, and a colleague who has seen the plan runs the other. After six minutes, bring everyone back to the main video call and compare the funniest drawing from each room.
