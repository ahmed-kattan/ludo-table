A language exchange through drawing: describe it, then draw it

Ahmed Moaz · 13 October 2026

When you are learning a language, the stuck moment is a missing word. A picture can stand in for it. A drawing game online turns that into practice: one partner draws, the other has to type the word in the language being learned. The plan below uses Draw and Guess with a short list of your own words, and it needs two people, 40 minutes and no preparation beyond writing a list. Two partners are enough, and a third or fourth learner can join to play with friends from the same class.

Step 1: describe each word out loud

Each partner writes twelve words in their own language. Choose words the other person will actually need: things in a kitchen, a street, a workplace. Pick words that can be drawn, so "colander" works and "although" does not.

Then take turns describing them without saying them. "It has lots of small holes. You pour pasta into it over the sink." The listener tries to name the word, in the language being learned, and the speaker corrects them. Give each word about a minute. Twelve words take roughly twelve minutes for each partner, so the two lists take about 25 minutes. The point of this step is that both of you know the list before the game starts.

Step 2: put the list in as custom words

In the lobby the host can type in their own words, with commas between them, each up to 32 characters. Two limits matter here. The lobby has a "Use custom words only" switch, and it needs at least 10 words; without it, yours are mixed into the built-in list. And the dealing code marks every word it offers a drawer as used, not just the one the drawer picks.

So the list has to be longer than you would guess. A drawer is offered three words by default. Two players over two rounds draw four times, which is 4 times 3, or 12 words used up. A list of ten would start repeating in the fourth turn. Use at least 12 words, or lower the number of words offered to two, which brings the need down to 8 and keeps the 10-word minimum as the real limit.

The box for your own word list is in the lobby of Boardit, and it opens in a browser for free.

Step 3: play two short games, one list each

Play the first partner's list, which is in their own language, then swap to the second partner's. Set two rounds and 60 seconds a drawing. Each turn runs at most 12 seconds to pick, 60 to draw, then 6 for the result, so four turns take at most 5 minutes 12 seconds. That is a ceiling, because a turn ends the moment the guesser has it.

The guesser types the word in the language being learned. The game accepts any capitals and ignores accents on letters such as é, so a missing accent is not marked wrong. That is a limit for a learner, because the game will not tell you that you forgot it. A near miss of a single letter does tell you, privately, that you are close, but only on words with four letters or more. For spelling practice that nudge is the useful part: you know the word is almost right, and you fix it yourself.

Settings that go easier on a learner

Points are paid for speed, and typing in a second language is slow. A right answer loses 5 points for every second of an 80-second drawing, so ten seconds hunting for a spelling costs 50. On 120 seconds the loss is about 3.3 points a second, so the same ten seconds costs about 33. For an exchange, the longer clock is the kinder choice.

Hints help in the same way. A host can allow up to three, each filling in a letter as the drawing goes on. None can give away more than half the word, so a four-letter word shows at most two. Switch hints to three, offer the drawer only two words, and the learner gets a steadier game with the same vocabulary list.

If scores start to matter more than the words, stop keeping them. The exchange works whether or not anyone wins.

Can you play Draw and Guess with only one language partner?

Yes. The game starts with two players: one draws and one guesses, then the pencil passes, so each of you draws in every round. With two people the table is quiet, which suits a language exchange because you can talk between turns. Add more players to the room if a class or club wants to take part, and add six words to the list for each extra player at two rounds.

Does drawing help you remember the word?

There is a general finding behind the idea. Wikipedia's article on the picture superiority effect, opened on 10 October 2026, describes it as the tendency for pictures to be remembered better than words. It credits dual-coding theory, from Paivio in 1971 and 1986: a picture is stored both visually and verbally, and a word mostly verbally.

That is a reason to try the plan, not proof it works. The finding concerns remembering pictures, and a rough sketch on a screen is a weak picture. This plan is untested.
