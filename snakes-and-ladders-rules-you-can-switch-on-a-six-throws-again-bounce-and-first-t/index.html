Snakes and ladders rules you can switch on: a six throws again, bounce and first to 3

Ahmed Moaz · 28 October 2026

The standard snakes and ladders rules leave the host with nothing to decide. On my table the host now has three switches, and I want to explain exactly what each one does, including the corners that people only find in play. All three are off by default, so a snakes and ladders game where the host touches nothing is the standard one.

What does a six throwing again change?

With this switch on, a six gives you another throw straight away. You move the six, resolve any ladder or snake, and then throw again on a fresh 30-second clock.

There is a limit. A turn can hold at most three throws. If you throw a six on the first and a six on the second, the third throw is yours. If that third throw is a six too, the counter still moves its six squares, and then the turn passes. Nobody gets a fourth.

One detail surprises people. The extra throw comes even if the six dropped you down a snake. The six is what buys the throw, not where you landed.

How does bounce work when you overshoot?

Without the switch, a throw that goes past 100 leaves your counter where it is. With bounce on, the counter walks up to 100 and then turns around for whatever is left.

The arithmetic is simple. Take the square you started on, add the throw, and subtract the total from 200. From 97 with a five, that is 200 minus 97 minus 5, which is 98. The counter touches 100 on the way up, but it comes to rest on 98.

On the Classic board, 98 is the head of a snake that slides to 78. So the bounce can drop you down a snake in the same move. A bounce never ends on 100, so it can never win the game. Only an exact throw does that.

What happens when the switches meet?

They combine in the way you would hope, and the edge cases are worth knowing. Say you stand on 97 with both the extra throw and bounce on, and you throw a six. The counter walks to 100 and turns back for the other three squares, so it ends on 97, exactly where it began. Because the throw was a six, you then throw again.

The Classic board adds a twist. From 96, a six takes you up to 100 and back to 98, and 98 is a snake head. You slide to 78 in the same move. Only the exact throws of 4 from 96 or 3 from 97 would have won. Each of these is a small story that the plain rules cannot produce.

Can a six win the game outright?

Yes. If a six lands you exactly on 100, the game is over at that moment. You do not get to use up the other throws in the turn, because the winning landing ends the game at once. The same holds for a ladder whose top is 100: on the Classic board that is the climb from 80, and reaching it ends the game just as a plain throw would.

What is a first-to-3 match?

Here the game stops being one race and becomes a match. The first player home wins that game, and everyone goes back to the start for the next one. The board is the same, and the score carries over. The match ends when one player has won three games.

The turn does not restart at seat one. It carries on to the next seat, so the player who starts each game changes as the match goes on. At the end the order is games won first. Ties are broken by how far up the final board each counter had got.

Should you switch them all on?

It depends on the evening. I think about it as three separate questions.

  • Want turns to feel more eventful? Choose the extra throw on a six.
  • Tired of waiting on 96 for a three? Choose bounce.
  • Want one result that feels earned over several games? Choose the match.

Nobody has timed these switches yet, me included, so I will not guess a number. My other figures for the Classic board, such as 39.23 throws for a single piece, describe the default rules only. With the extra throw and bounce both on, I would expect shorter games, because pieces travel further each turn. That is reasoning, not a measurement.

Setting the rules for snakes and ladders online

On Boardit the host sets all three in the lobby.

For two players a first-to-3 match is a tidy way to settle who gets the better seat next time. For a table of eight, I would leave it at one game. A match of three or more games with that many people is a long evening.

Which combination for which night?

  • A quick game with friends between two bigger ones: leave everything off.
  • A relaxed multiplayer evening: six throws again and bounce.
  • A small tournament: first to 3, nothing else, so the result is easy to explain.
