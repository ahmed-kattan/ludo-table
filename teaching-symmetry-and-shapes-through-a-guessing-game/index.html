Teaching symmetry and shapes through a guessing game

Ahmed Moaz · 12 October 2026

A guessing game can teach symmetry because the drawer has to show a shape's structure in seconds and the guessers have to name it. This post gives a 16-word list of shape and symmetry vocabulary, a recording sheet, and two ways to run it: on paper, or in Draw and Guess, a free online drawing game I make, which groups also play with friends outside lessons. That game is made for players of 13 and older, so I would use the online version with older learners or adult classes, and paper with anyone younger. I make no claim that it suits any particular classroom. This is an activity idea.

Sixteen words that carry the lesson

Reflection symmetry means a figure stays unchanged when it is reflected, as the Wikipedia article on reflection symmetry puts it. The list below mixes shapes that have it with shapes that do not, so learners have to decide.

square, rectangle, circle, kite, rhombus, parallelogram, trapezium, isosceles triangle, equilateral triangle, right angle, hexagon, octagon, line of symmetry, reflection, rotation, perpendicular lines.

For the recording sheet, give learners seven shapes and a blank box beside each. They write how many lines of symmetry the shape has. The answers I would check against are: square 4, rectangle that is not a square 2, equilateral triangle 3, regular hexagon 6, regular octagon 8, kite 1, and circle endless. The article confirms the square and the circle. A parallelogram that is not a rectangle or a rhombus has none, which is the answer that surprises people.

Running it on paper

Work in pairs. One learner draws a word from the list for 80 seconds while the other guesses. A right guess scores a point for the guesser, and a second point goes to the drawer if the guess came inside half the time. Then both learners mark any lines of symmetry in a second colour and say how many they found.

The second colour is where the learning happens. Drawing a rhombus is quick. Explaining why it has two lines of symmetry and a square has four is the lesson. A good prompt is to ask each pair which shape surprised them, and why.

Running it in Draw and Guess

The host can type the list into the custom words box. With ten or more different words, the host can switch on custom words only, and the list stays hidden from the rest of the table. Each turn, the game offers the drawer a few words, three by default, and marks every offered word as used, not just the one picked. So 16 words cover only five full turns at three offers: the sixth turn has one unused word left, so the game deals from the whole list again and words repeat.

Set the offer to two words instead. A table of four learners drawing for two rounds is 8 turns, and 8 turns at two words each is exactly 16. Every word is offered once.

Spelling gets practice too. A typo of a single letter earns the typist a private "close" notice, though only on words of four letters or longer. So "isoceles" for "isosceles" gets a quiet nudge. Capitals and accents do not matter, and a hyphen counts as a space.

For the drawer, the drawing tools include a straight line and four shapes: circle, square, rectangle and triangle. A good tip for a line of symmetry is to draw the axis first, then one half, then mirror it. Hints fill in letters as time passes, never more than half the word, so a long word like "perpendicular lines" gives itself away slowly. That makes the longest words on the list the hardest, which is a useful thing to know before you write it.
