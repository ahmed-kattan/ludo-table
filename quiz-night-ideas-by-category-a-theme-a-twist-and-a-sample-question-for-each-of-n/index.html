Quiz night ideas by category: a theme, a twist and a sample question for each of nine subjects

Ahmed Moaz · 6 November 2026

A quiz night needs more than a pile of trivia questions. It needs a reason to turn up. The theme is that reason, and it can be as small as a dress code or a snack. These quiz night ideas follow the nine categories in Trivia Tavern, so every theme below is something you can actually pick in the lobby for an online trivia evening with friends. I have added a twist for each, which is only my suggestion for how to dress the evening.

How many themes make an evening?

Pick three. A pub-quiz guide recommends five to eight rounds of eight to ten questions, but a game on phones is a different animal. My standard match has three rounds of ten questions, which takes about 14 minutes. Rounds can be set to 1, 3, 5 or 7, and the guide's point still stands: themed rounds add memorable variety.

So choose three themes, one for each round, and put the lightest first. That ordering matters more than the subjects.

Which theme suits each category?

Here is a menu. Each entry names the category to tick, a theme to announce, and a twist for the room.

General knowledge: "Anything goes." The safest opener, with no specialist advantage. The twist is a forfeit for the first wrong answer of the night, kept friendly: the loser picks the next song on the call.

Entertainment: "Screen night." Tick it for films and TV. Ask everyone to arrive as a character from their favourite show, and hold a vote at the end.

Music: "Name that decade." Ask each guest to bring a track from the year they were fifteen, and play them in the gaps between rounds.

Sports: "Armchair champions." Open with a laugh by asking who has never played anything. Here is a taste of the questions. What is the highest score you can get from three darts? It is 180, which is three treble 20s.

Geography: "Passport check." Each guest names a place they want to visit. The twist is to hold up a finger on the call each time a country you have been to comes up. Here is another sample. In which city is the Atomium, a giant model of an iron crystal? Brussels, built for the 1958 World's Fair.

History: "Time travellers." Ask people to say which century they would visit. It sets the mood for questions that run from ancient to modern.

Science and nature: "Curious minds." Good for a mixed-age family, because children can surprise the adults on animals and space. The twist is a "stump the grown-ups" rule: a child is allowed to ask the room a question during the break.

Food and drink: "Supper club." Everyone eats the same thing, or each guest cooks one dish from a different country. Here is a sample. Which Italian blue cheese is named after a town near Milan? Gorgonzola.

Video games: "Player two." The theme for a younger crowd, or for friends who play. The twist is to split the room into two generations and see which one scores higher.

All three samples can be checked on Wikipedia in a minute, and every one of them has a place on a night that includes it.

How do you order the three rounds?

Use a simple shape: a warm-up, a middle and a finale.

  1. A broad opener, such as General knowledge or Food and drink, where everyone scores.
  2. A themed middle round, with a category your group loves.
  3. A closer that suits your sharpest players, because the final round pays twice.

That last point is a design choice you can lean on. A player who is behind has a real chance in the final round, so the themed closer can be bold without feeling unkind.

Should you tell guests the themes in advance?

Yes, in the invite. A theme with a prop attached gets people to join in, and a prop costs nothing: a paper hat, a snack, a song to bring. If you tell people the three themes, they can arrive in the right mood. If you leave one secret, you get the pleasure of a surprise round.

What if your group does not share one theme?

Fall back on Mixed. The lobby then draws from all nine categories in equal shares, and the evening has a theme of "everything". It is not a failure. Plenty of good quiz night ideas need no preparation at all.

How do you pull it together?

Boardit's Trivia Tavern takes the categories in a few taps, and the match seats two to eight. Open the lobby, tick the three subjects for your running order, and share the invite. A trivia quiz with a theme feels like an event, and a trivia night with three of them feels planned. The questions, the clock and the scores are handled, which leaves you free to run the theme.

Then keep it light. A theme should make the room laugh within the first five minutes, and everything after that is a bonus.
