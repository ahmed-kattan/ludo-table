Quiet colleagues and loud games: remote team games for introverts

Ahmed Moaz · 17 October 2026

Most remote team games reward whoever speaks first. Buzz-in quizzes, round-robin icebreakers and "tell us one fun fact" all hand the floor to the fastest talker and put everyone else on the spot. Remote team games for introverts do not have to be silent. They need three things: typed answers, everyone answering at once, and speaking left optional. I build Boardit, a small site of multiplayer browser games, and its six games pass that test to very different degrees.

Waiting your turn costs ideas

Psychologists have a name for part of the problem. In 1987, Michael Diehl and Wolfgang Stroebe compared brainstorming groups with the same number of people working alone, in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Across four experiments, they found that "production blocking", having to wait for others before you can speak, explained most of what the groups lost.

A game round works the same way. While you wait for a gap, your answer goes stale or someone else says it. The person least willing to interrupt pays most of that cost.

Two games with no queue

Two of Boardit's games remove the queue entirely.

In Trivia Tavern, everyone answers the same question at the same moment, each on a separate phone. There is no buzzing in, nobody waits for a turn, and every player answers every question. A slow, careful colleague is barely punished either: by default a right answer keeps at least three quarters of its points, however long it takes.

In Draw and Guess, guesses are typed into the chat built into the game, so nobody has to speak at all. So people who would rather not speak on a call can still win it.

Where the spotlight still falls

Neither game is pressure-free, and it is worth knowing where the spotlight lands.

In Draw and Guess, everyone draws once a round while the whole table watches the picture form. For someone who hates being watched, that turn is the hard part, microphone or not. Wrong guesses are also shown to everyone, and quicker correct guesses earn more, which favours fast typists over slow ones who knew first.

Who's the Spy is the opposite of what you might expect. Online, clues are typed, a short phrase of up to 20 characters with 25 seconds each, but they circle the table in laps. Everyone takes a turn, and the whole game is about being suspected. That makes it a good game to start with, and one that rewards people who are quiet in meetings. For a team with several quiet people, I would still start with the quiz.

Running remote team games for introverts

A few rules make speaking genuinely optional:

  • Say that the game is optional, out loud, and mean it.
  • Start with the quiz. It seats two to eight, and a bigger team can split into rooms and compare scores.
  • For nine to twelve people in one room, Draw and Guess is the only one of the six that fits.
  • Keep the call for talk, not for the game. If nobody speaks for a whole round, the game still works.

The games I make for calls like this run in a browser at playboardit.com.

What typing can't fix

Typing does not make a game kind. Scores are still public, and a last place is visible to the whole team. Quiet is also not the same as shy, and some colleagues simply do not want a game, or a team game night, at work, whatever its format. I have no data on how introverts feel playing these games. This is a reading of the rules, not a study.
