The world wars in a quiz: handling sensitive history fairly

Ahmed Moaz · 16 October 2026

A trivia quiz can include the world wars, both World War I and World War II, fairly if every question has a settled answer, no answer option makes light of the dead, and the table agrees to the topic first. That is a short list, but it rules out a lot of what people reach for. The usual question, how many died, is the first casualty. Here is why, and what to write instead.

Which trivia questions about the world wars are fair to ask?

The fair ones ask about events that historians do not argue over: which treaty, which campaign, which year, which person, which place. Questions with a contested answer, a total that depends on method, or a verdict about who was to blame are not fair. A wrong answer should cost points, not dignity, and a right answer should never feel like a celebration of the war itself.

Why the death toll is the wrong question

Look at how public summaries handle it. Wikipedia's article on Second World War casualties gives an estimate of 60 to 75 million deaths including famine and disease, while its own summary table runs from 70 to 85 million. It says estimates differ because methods differ, and because the line between military and civilian deaths is not always clear. The article on the First World War gives about 15 to 22 million deaths, and warns that its figures cannot be considered comparable in all cases.

A multiple-choice question needs one correct option. Where the sources span tens of millions, such as 60 to 85 million for the Second World War, there is no single number to put in the answer. Any option you choose will be wrong by someone's method. A question of that kind teaches that history is a score.

I read the World wars topic in the question bank of the game I build for playing trivia online on 10 October 2026. It has 100 questions, 40 easy, 40 medium and 20 hard. None asks for a casualty total. The questions ask which place, campaign, person or code name. Each carries a reveal line and at least one source link kept with the question, and 88 have exactly one.

Wrong answers are part of the design

On a four-option question, three options are wrong, and what they are matters. The question writers' brief for this quiz says the four options must be the same kind of thing, all plausible, with no trick questions. That means four years, four cities or four treaties. With war history I would add one rule: make the wrong options places, dates or names of operations, never peoples. A question that asks which group did something terrible, with other groups as distractors, makes a player guess at blame.

Whose war does the quiz tell?

Fairness is also about coverage. The wars were global, and Wikipedia's casualty article notes that famine and disease deaths make up a large share of the toll in China and the Soviet Union. So I searched the questions, answers and wrong options of the World wars topic for places, by keyword, on 10 October 2026. The Soviet Union or Russia appears in 13 of the 100. Japan or the Pacific appears in 15. China appears in none; it shows up only once, in a reveal line.

That is a gap, and not a scandal; 100 questions cannot cover everything, and the bank is not a history course. But anyone who writes history trivia for a mixed table should count the same way. A quiz that only visits one front quietly tells one country's story.

Tell the table before you start

Someone at a table, even a table of friends, may have family history that the others do not know about. The host picks topics in the lobby, so the simplest courtesy is to say which ones are in the quiz before the first question. Players can also stay silent on a single question. An unanswered question costs only its points, and it takes five misses in a row, while others keep answering, before a player is removed from the match.

I would play this topic with friends who know it is coming, not spring it on a mixed group such as a work party.

The reveal line after each answer helps. It gives the answer a reason, and since every question was written against a source, a disputed answer can be checked afterwards instead of argued over.

One question, written two ways

A weak version: How many people died in the Second World War? The sources above give two overlapping ranges, so every option misleads.

A fair version, freshly written for this piece: In which year did the Armistice that ended fighting on the Western Front take effect, on 11 November? The answer options are 1916, 1917, 1918 and 1919. The answer is 1918.
