A property-game night for six: seating, snacks and a hard stop

Ahmed Moaz · 14 October 2026

A property-game night for six needs three decisions before anyone sits down: where people sit, what they eat, and when the game stops. The third is the one that gets forgotten. Property Rush is a property trading game that you play online with friends, and it has no round limit, so the stop is something your table agrees on, not something the game enforces. This plan gives you seats, snacks and a 45-minute schedule with a count at the end.

Why plan for six

Six is the most seats a table can have. A full table is livelier, because more people means more trades and more auctions. It also means more waiting. Between your turns, five other people roll, buy, bid and bargain.

The site lists the game at 45 minutes. The guide adds a caution: seats, auctions, trades and house rules all move that figure, and each player has a three-minute bank to spend on top of a minute a turn. So 45 minutes is a plan to aim at, not a promise. I have no measured length for a six-player game, and I would rather say so than invent one.

Seating when everyone has a screen

Each player brings a phone or laptop, so nobody needs to sit where a shared screen can be seen. That frees you to seat people for conversation. Trades are usually negotiated by talking, and the people who will bargain most should be able to hear each other without shouting.

The first roller is drawn at random unless the host turns the shuffle off, so seating does not set who plays when. Do not seat the host at the head of the table out of habit. A round table, or a cluster of sofas, makes the haggling easier.

Count power points. Six phones or laptops need six chargers, or six well-charged batteries, for an evening that may run past the hour.

Remote friends fit in the same way. One person on a video call plays on equal terms with the people in the room, because everybody has their own device. Just make sure the call is audible, because the bargaining is half the game.

Snacks that do not touch the screens

The rule for food is simple: nothing that leaves a mark on a phone. Pick dry, one-handed food, such as crackers, fruit, nuts and pretzels, and put it in bowls in the middle of the table, not on laps. Lidded cups prevent spilled drinks over a keyboard.

There is a game reason, too. A player who stops to wipe their hands is a player who is not moving, and the turn clock does not care. Each turn has a minute, then a three-minute bank that has to last the whole game; when both run out, a bot finishes the turn safely and buys nothing. Food that needs both hands is how a friend loses the city they were about to buy.

A 45-minute plan with a hard stop

Here is the running order I would use. The numbers add up to 5 + 5 + 25 + 5 + 5 = 45.

MinutesWhat happens
0 to 5Seat everyone, open the table, check the starting purse
5 to 10First lap: buy freely, learn the auctions
10 to 35The trading window: swaps, builds and bargains
35 to 40The host calls the last lap
40 to 45The count, the result, a handshake

Choose the starting purse first

The host chooses the opening purse in the lobby, anywhere between $500 and $5,000. The default is $1,500. The guide's advice for a shorter evening is to start smaller, because money runs short sooner. Six players at $1,500 start with $9,000 between them. At $500 they start with $3,000, a third as much. With less money in play, rents bite earlier.

If your night has a hard stop, go smaller than the default.

The count at the hard stop

The game does not end at minute 40, so your table has to. Agree this rule before the first roll: when the host calls the end, each player adds their cash to the printed price of every city they own. The biggest total wins. Ties go to the player with more cash.

Take a worked count, using the board's own prices. Dana has $410 in cash and owns Kyoto (220), Osaka (240) and Tokyo (210). Her total is 410 + 220 + 240 + 210 = 1,080. Sam has $900 in cash and owns Rome (160) and Milan (140). His total is 900 + 160 + 140 = 1,200. Sam wins, even though Dana owns more cities.

That rule is mine, not the game's. It ignores buildings, and it does not say what to do about mortgaged cities, so decide both in advance. A count only works if nobody argues about it afterwards.

If the game finishes early

Sometimes one player is left before minute 35. That is the game's own ending, and the night's winner is decided. Use the spare time for a second, shorter game with a $500 purse.

Where to check the rules
