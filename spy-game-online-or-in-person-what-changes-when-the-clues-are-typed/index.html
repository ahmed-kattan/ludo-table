Spy game online or in person? What changes when the clues are typed

Ahmed Moaz · 21 October 2026

You can play Who's the Spy in two ways. Everyone on their own device, typing clues, or everyone around a room, passing one phone and speaking. They are the same game with different strengths, and the choice between them is worth making on purpose. Here is how a spy game online differs from the in-person version, mode by mode.

What stays the same?

The heart: a secret word for everyone but the spy, short clues in turn, a vote and points. The spy is told only the category. A caught lone spy guesses from ten words. Nobody is eliminated. Three rounds by default. The score is kept for you.

What changes about the clues?

Online, they are typed, limited to twenty characters and twenty-five seconds. In person, they are spoken, with no fixed length except what the table tolerates. Typed clues are cleaner: no tone of voice, no pause, no flicker of the face. Spoken clues carry far more information about the speaker, which is good for detectives and bad for spies.

What changes about the vote?

Online, a vote opens when more than half the table asks for it within a ten-second window after every two laps. In person, the host decides when to vote. The online way is fairer to quieter players; the in-person way is quicker and relies on the host's judgement.

How many can play?

Online, three to eight. In person on one phone, three to twelve. A big family or a classroom suits the shared phone; a group of friends in different places suits the online table.

What do you gain online?

Distance. Friends in different cities can play. Privacy: a typed word is hidden on your own screen, so there is no phone to peek at. Automatic clocks: nobody has to time the clues. And cleaner evidence, which makes the game more about reasoning than about acting.

What do you gain in person?

Faces. Reading a friend's reaction to a clue is half the fun for people who like to watch. A room adds laughter, nudges and the noise of a crowd. It also needs only one device, so nobody has to be a good typist.

Which is harder for the spy?

In person. A spoken clue exposes hesitation, an odd tone or a look at the ceiling. Online, the spy can take twenty-five seconds in private to think. So a spy game online is a little kinder to the bluffer and a little harder for the detective.

How do you choose for a given evening?

Ask three questions. Is everyone in one place? If not, play online. Does everyone have a phone and a free hand? If not, share one. Is the group larger than eight? If so, use the shared phone, which takes twelve. A fourth question decides the mood: do you want a calm, thoughtful game, or a loud one? Typed clues give you the first, spoken clues the second.

Most groups end up using both, online for the weeknights and the shared phone for the weekends.

Can you combine them?

Yes. A room with one shared phone can be one seat at an online table with other friends joining remotely. Let the room confer on its clue. It makes a team of the room and keeps everyone involved.

Where can you try both?

At Boardit, a free imposter game online with friends, the host chooses the mode when setting up the table. Try one game each way and ask the group which they preferred.

A final suggestion

Use online for distance, in person for an evening together. The rules are the same; the mood is not.
