Two-person teams in a quiz: rules for fair pairing

Ahmed Moaz · 16 October 2026

The fairest way to make two-person teams for a trivia quiz is to rank everyone with a short warm-up and then pair the strongest with the weakest, the second strongest with the second weakest, and so on. That keeps every pair's combined strength close. Below is the arithmetic, plus rules for who holds the phone and what a pair costs in points at a trivia night.

The pairing rule, with eight players

Say eight friends answer ten warm-up trivia questions alone, whether the trivia night is online or in a living room. The scores, which are made up for this example, are 9, 8, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2 and 1.

Pair neighbours and you get 9+8, 6+5, 4+3 and 2+1. The pair totals are 17, 11, 7 and 3. The first team is more than five times as strong as the last, and the last team will spend an evening knowing it.

Pair from the two ends and you get 9+1, 8+2, 6+3 and 5+4. The pair totals are 10, 10, 9 and 9. Every team now has about the same chance, and every pair has one person who can carry a topic the other cannot.

That is the rule: rank, then fold the list in half.

Rank by a warm-up, not by reputation

Do not ask people how good they are. The confident ones say very good and the careful ones say not much. Ten questions answered alone, with no talking, take a few minutes and give you a number.

Use mixed categories for the warm-up. Someone who is strong on films and weak on science will pair better with a person who is the other way round, and a mixed set of general knowledge questions shows that. Keep the warm-up out of the scoring for the night, so nobody feels the ranking is a verdict.

Who holds the phone

In Trivia Tavern each seat answers alone, once per question. There is no team mode, so a pair shares one seat and one phone, and a wrong tap cannot be taken back.

Two rules keep this fair:

  • The weaker partner of each pair holds the phone for the first round. The stronger one advises. It keeps the weaker person involved from the start.
  • Swap the phone each round. Over three rounds, each person taps for at least one.

The partner who is not tapping may say one answer out loud before the tap, and then only one. A running argument costs points, as the next section shows.

What a pair costs in points

On the default 20-second clock, a right answer keeps all its points if it is instant and loses at most a quarter if it lands at the end, on a straight line. That is 1.25 percent of the question's points for every second used. A wrong answer scores nothing.

Here is a worked example. A solo player answers in 4 seconds. A pair debates and answers in 12.

The pair gives away 20 to 30 points a question for talking. To come out ahead, they need to be right more often. On a 200-point question, 190 divided by 170 is 1.12, so a pair needs to be about 12 percent more accurate than a solo player just to break even. A solo player right 60 percent of the time is matched by a pair right about 67 percent of the time. If your partners are well matched, they will get more than that. If they are not, they will not.

So pairs pay off when the partners cover each other's gaps, and not otherwise. The numbers assume a four-second solo answer and a twelve-second pair answer; a real table will differ.

Give pairs a longer clock

The host can set the answer clock to 10, 15, 20 or 30 seconds before the game starts. For pairs, 30 is worth choosing, because the cost of a second falls with it. On 30 seconds a second costs about 0.83 percent of a question's points instead of 1.25.

The pair's penalty for talking is cut in half. On the 30-second clock, 190 divided by 180 is about 1.06, so a pair needs only about 6 percent better accuracy to break even. The cost is a slower game, since an answer clock only ends early once every seat has answered.

What to do with an odd number

Nine friends make four pairs and one player left over. There are two honest options.

The odd player can play alone against the pairs. They answer fast and keep more points per right answer, but they have no partner to catch a slip.

Or the odd player can join a pair as a trio. A trio takes longer to agree, and that is where the clock bites: a trio that needs 16 seconds on the 20-second clock keeps 160 of 200 points, against 190 for a solo answer in four seconds. On the 30-second clock it keeps 170. Trios are tolerable only with the longer clock.

Neither is perfect. Pick one before the warm-up and do not change it mid-game.

Let the categories help

The host picks which categories come up, or leaves it on Mixed, which gives every category an equal share. For pairs, Mixed is the right choice. The fold-in-half pairing is built so that each pair has a person who can carry what the other cannot, and an equal share of every category is what lets that pay off. A table limited to one category rewards whichever pair happens to hold the specialist.

A checklist for the night

  • Run the warm-up alone and in silence.
  • Pair from the two ends of the ranking.
  • Weaker partner taps first, and swap each round.
  • One answer said aloud, then tap.
  • Choose the 30-second clock and Mixed categories.
  • Decide how to handle an odd player before the warm-up.
  • Leave ties to the game's own tiebreak.

A table of Trivia Tavern seats two to eight, which in pairs means up to sixteen people, and you can find the game on Boardit. The shape of the evening is the same for trivia online with friends or around a living room.
