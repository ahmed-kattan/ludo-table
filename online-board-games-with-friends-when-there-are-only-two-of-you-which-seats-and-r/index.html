Online board games with friends when there are only two of you: which seats and rules suit a pair

Ahmed Moaz · 30 October 2026

Plenty of lists of online board games with friends assume five or six chairs are filled. Those lists are of little use on a quiet night in. Reality is often one friend, one evening and a video call. Some games get better with two, some get worse, and one will not start at all. Here is how my six free games behave when the table has exactly two seats filled.

Which games will not work for two?

Only one. Who's the Spy needs at least three people online, because the spy needs a few people who know the word to hide among. If you want it, wait for a third friend, or play it on one shared phone, where it takes up to twelve. Every other game starts with two.

How does Ludo feel with two players?

Ludo takes two to four. With two, the board is emptier, so captures are rarer. If your piece stands one to six squares ahead of an opponent's, only one number on the die hits it, a chance of one in six, and with a single opponent there are fewer pieces within reach. The game becomes more of a race.

If a race is the wrong mood, the host can choose Quick Ludo: each player gets two pieces and a game lasts around five minutes. That is a pleasant pocket-sized game for a lunch break. For a longer evening, the full game with four pieces each runs thirty to sixty minutes.

Is Snakes and Ladders fair for two?

Nearly. In my simulation of the rules, whoever throws first wins 50.8 percent of two-player games on the classic board, against an even 50 percent. That edge is tiny, and swapping who goes first for a rematch cancels it. On the same board, the expected number of rounds before somebody finishes is about 26.

The host can make the board kinder or crueller. A ladder-heavy layout speeds things up, a snake-heavy one slows them down, and a match played to three wins turns a ten-minute game into a proper contest.

What about Property Rush with two?

A property trading game is at its sharpest with two people, because there is nobody else to trade with and nobody to blame. Every rent you collect comes straight out of your friend's pocket. It takes two to six players, with no limit on the number of rounds: the last player with money wins.

To keep it short, lower the starting purse. Starting cash can be set anywhere between $500 and $5,000. A small purse makes every purchase a big decision and ends the evening sooner.

Do the quiz and the drawing game work for a pair?

Yes, with a caveat for each. Trivia Tavern runs two to eight players. With two, the quiz becomes a duel, and the closing question that rewards the nearest number can decide it. If you cannot find a second player, there is a solo mode as well.

Draw and Guess welcomes anywhere from two to twelve players. With two, one of you draws and the other guesses, so the guesser carries the scoring on every drawing. When only one person can guess, the drawer is paid whatever that one guess earned, so a fast guess pays both of you. Lengthen the drawing time and turn the hints up, so that neither of you feels rushed.

Can you add a computer-played seat?

In Ludo, Snakes and Ladders and Property Rush the host can fill free seats with computer-played ones. This is useful for Ludo, which plays fullest with four. It is not a substitute for your friend, but it keeps a board lively. Trivia, Who's the Spy and Draw and Guess have no such seats.

How do you pick for the evening?

  • Fifteen minutes to spare: Quick Ludo or the quiz.
  • An hour: a property trading game with a small purse.
  • Mostly talking, a little playing: the drawing game, since it needs almost no thought.
  • Looking for a rematch: Snakes and Ladders, which resets in minutes.

I keep all six on Boardit, free and with no download, so you and your friend can change games whenever you like.

What I would do first

Play the shortest game, change the settings if it felt wrong, and then pick a long one. A two-player game night wants a warm-up more than a big table does.
