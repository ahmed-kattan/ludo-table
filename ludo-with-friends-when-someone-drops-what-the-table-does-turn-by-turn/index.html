Ludo with friends when someone drops: what the table does, turn by turn

Ahmed Moaz · 31 October 2026

Anyone who has played ludo with friends over the internet knows the moment. It is somebody's turn, and nothing happens. Their signal has gone, or the phone call has taken over, or the battery has died. Three other people wait. I have thought hard about this moment, because a ludo game that stalls on one absent player is a bad game. Here is what Boardit's Ludo does, step by step, from the first missed throw to the friend coming back.

What happens in the first thirty seconds?

Every turn runs on a 30-second clock, the same for all players. For half a minute the table simply waits. If the person throws and moves inside it, the clock is irrelevant, and being slow but present never counts against you.

What does the clock play for a missing player?

When the 30 seconds pass, the table throws the die and moves for them. It does not skip the turn, and that is deliberate. A player who is never dealt a throw can never get the six they need to leave base, so skipping would make the game unwinnable for them.

The move it makes follows a fixed order. It takes an opponent's piece if it can. Failing that, it brings a piece home. Failing that, it brings one out of base. Failing that, it moves the piece furthest along. It is not clever, but it is the order most people would choose with a glance at the board.

When does a bot take the seat?

After two turns in a row played by the clock. One missed turn is someone fetching a drink. Two in a row is someone who is not there. At that point a bot plays the seat, and the log says so for everyone.

The seat does not change hands. The absent player's pieces stay theirs on the ludo board, and so does their place in the finishing order. The bot only makes the throws and moves. Nobody is thrown out for a dropped connection.

What happens when the friend comes back?

They open the table again and take the seat back, or they simply play a move of their own. Either way the bot steps out at once, and the next turn is theirs. This works at any point before the game ends, however long they were gone.

I should be honest about one thing. A bot plays the seat well, so the position may look different from the one your friend left. That is part of the cost of a game that keeps moving. If your group would rather pause, agree that in the call, because the table itself will not wait.

Can the others remove a player who never returns?

Yes, by a vote, though I would use it rarely. The number of votes needed is the larger of two and the number of players minus two, counting everyone still in the game. With three players that is two votes, and with four players it is also two. A vote stays open for 90 seconds. In a two-player game nobody can vote, since one player can simply resign.

Most of the time you will not need it. A covered seat keeps the game moving, and a friend who has gone for the night leaves their pieces on the board without blocking anyone.

How do you keep the game friendly?

Say it out loud before you start: if someone drops, the bot plays, and they take the seat back when they return. You can try ludo online with that agreement on Boardit's Ludo, which I build. Then nobody feels rushed to rejoin, and nobody feels abandoned.

When you are the one who dropped

Open the table again before anything else. The ludo rules have not changed, your pieces are where you left them, and your seat is waiting.
